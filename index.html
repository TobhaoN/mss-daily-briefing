Automakers sue New Jersey over dealer warranty and recall repair payments

Ford, Honda, Stellantis' FCA unit and Volkswagen filed in federal court in Trenton on Nov. 20 to block the New Jersey Motor Vehicle Open Recall Notice and Fair Compensation Act, which took effect April 1. The automakers say it lets dealers set reimbursement prices for warranty, recall and other repairs and estimate it shifts more than $375 million a year from manufacturers to dealers. They argue it violates the Commerce and Takings clauses, conflicts with federal recall rules, and breaches the state constitution. The New Jersey dealer coalition is a named defendant and disputes the claims.

What it means for us: warranty and recall reimbursement is the quiet engine under a lot of fixed-ops profit, and several states have already tilted these rates toward dealers. If New Jersey holds, service-bay economics improve and the Xtime conversation gets easier. Either way, expect NJ dealers to be watching this closely while they plan 2027 service capacity.

The risk of letting AI do too much in the dealership

Bob Burgin of Amplifire argues on Inside Automotive that as AI moves from answering questions to executing workflows, the real risk is sales teams losing the judgment to manage it. He describes building an intelligence layer on top of a CRM using 14 specialized research sub-agents, and makes the case for designing AI to sharpen employee performance rather than becoming an easy button.

What it means for us: this is the counterweight to every agentic-AI pitch our competitors are making, and it is a better frame for our conversations than feature lists. The product that raises a rep's judgment beats the one that replaces it, and that is a defensible position for a connected platform with coaching and reporting built in.

Talk track: “The question isn't whether AI can do the follow-up. It's whether your people still know how to when it's wrong.”

Competitor Watch

Bidbus aims to upend vehicle auctions by letting dealers bid on consumers' cars

Bidbus runs a short live auction on consumer-owned vehicles, requiring dealers to bid against each other rather than the seller taking a single instant offer. It launched in 2022, operates in California and Texas with about 500 franchised dealerships, and charges a 1% success fee instead of listing or auction fees.

What it means for us: this attacks the instant-offer model head on, which is the lane KBB Instant Cash Offer sits in, and it pulls private-party supply toward a bidding war instead of toward the dealer's own appraisal. Still small and two-state, but worth knowing the model when a dealer says a customer walked in with a higher number from somewhere else.

OpenAI is testing Dots personal agents on its $20 per month Plus tier

OpenAI began testing Dots, its always-on background agents, with a small number of ChatGPT Plus subscribers on Nov. 19, and opened Dots to all ChatGPT Business Standard users the same day. Dots launched at DevDay in September for Pro and Business Premium. Each runs on its own cloud computer and keeps working while the user is away. They originally ran on GPT-6 Astra and can now run on GPT-6.1 Terra.

What it means for us: always-on agents just dropped to the $20 tier and to every US Business Standard seat, which means dealership staff will start using unsanctioned background agents on store data without anyone approving it. That is the same data-ownership problem QoreAI was warning dealers about last week, and it is worth raising before IT finds out the hard way.
